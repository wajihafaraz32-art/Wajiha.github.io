Of all the subjects I took in my first semester, Programming Fundamentals is the one that I think shaped my early university experience the most. It was the subject that forced me to confront, almost immediately, just how unfamiliar I was with the technical side of Computer Engineering, and it was also the subject that ended up teaching me the most about persistence.

Our instructor for the course was Dr. Bilal Ahmad, who also went on to teach us Database Management Systems in my second semester. In those first classes, he introduced us to the basics of programming, walked us through the course learning outcomes, and laid out the overall structure of what we would be covering over the term. I remember sitting in those early lectures trying to take in as much as possible, fully aware that I was starting from a much more basic level than many of the people around me.

At first, almost everything about the subject felt difficult. Concepts like algorithms, logic building, and the actual process of writing code did not come naturally to me the way I imagine they might for someone who had been exposed to programming earlier in life. I distinctly remember sitting with simple exercises and feeling like I was missing some fundamental piece of understanding that everyone else already had. It was not a comfortable feeling, especially in those first few weeks when everything still felt new and slightly overwhelming.

What gradually shifted my perspective was realizing that programming was not actually about memorizing syntax or copying patterns I had seen before. It was about developing a particular way of thinking, one centered on breaking a problem down into smaller, logical steps and then translating that structure into code. Once I started to internalize that idea, even slowly, the subject began to feel less like an arbitrary set of rules and more like a skill I could actually build over time with consistent effort.

I want to be honest that this shift in understanding did not happen quickly. There were many sessions where I left class still confused about a concept, only for it to click days later after enough repetition and practice. I think this is a normal part of learning something genuinely new, but it did not feel normal in the moment. It felt like I was falling behind, even when I was actually making steady, if slow, progress.

Looking back, I think the most valuable thing I took from this early stage of Programming Fundamentals was not any specific piece of syntax or any particular algorithm. It was the realization that struggling with a concept early on does not mean you are incapable of understanding it. It usually just means you have not yet found the right way to think about it, and that comes with time and repetition rather than instant clarity.
