I think it is worth dedicating a full post to just how difficult learning programming was for me in the beginning, because I do not think people who already had some coding background before university can fully understand what that experience feels like from the outside. Since I had no previous programming exposure at all, even the most basic syntax and the underlying logic of writing a program felt foreign to me in those early weeks.

There were entire sessions where I would sit with what was, by most standards, a simple program, and I would still spend hours trying to fully understand why it worked the way it did. It was not that I could not read the code on the page. It was that I could not yet see the underlying logic that connected each line to the next, the reasoning that more experienced programmers seem to apply almost automatically. That gap between reading code and actually understanding the thinking behind it was, for me, the hardest part of this entire stage.

Many concepts felt genuinely confusing in those first weeks, and I will admit that I often found myself doubting whether I had the ability to succeed in this field at all. This is not an easy thing to write publicly, but I think it is an important part of being honest about this journey. There were moments where I compared myself to classmates who seemed to grasp things instantly, and that comparison did not help my confidence. It made me question, more than once, whether I had made the right decision in choosing this field.

What eventually started to shift things for me was not any single breakthrough moment, but rather the slow accumulation of regular practice combined with guidance from both teachers and classmates. I leaned on my peers more than I expected to during this period. Asking a classmate to explain a concept in their own words, in a more casual setting than a lecture hall, often helped things click in a way that the formal explanation alone had not. I also made a habit of going back to instructors with questions rather than letting confusion sit unresolved, even when it felt uncomfortable to admit I was stuck on something basic.

Slowly, and I do mean slowly, I started improving. There was no dramatic turning point where everything suddenly made sense. Instead, it was a gradual process of small concepts clicking into place one at a time, building on each other until the bigger picture started to feel more manageable. Looking back, I think this gradual nature of progress is something I wish someone had explained to me earlier, because at the time, the lack of an obvious breakthrough made me feel like I was not actually improving at all, even when I was.

This period taught me an important lesson about learning anything genuinely new: the absence of instant understanding does not mean an absence of progress. Sometimes progress is quiet and difficult to notice in the moment, and it only becomes visible when you look back after enough time has passed.
